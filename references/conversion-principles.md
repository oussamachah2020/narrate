# Conversion principles and page structures

Distilled from landing-page conversion practice (including the HubSpot Hacks landing-page notes). Use these to plan the page before writing.

## Characteristics of a page that converts

1. **One CTA.** One primary action, repeated, with the same wording each time.
2. **Remove doors.** Fewer links and a lighter menu on campaign pages, so visitors aren't pulled away.
3. **One audience.** Write for one targeted reader, not "everyone".
4. **Answer the 5 W's** (below).

## The 5 W's: where to answer each

| Visitor's question         | What they are really asking                       | Where it goes                                |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Who are you?            | Can I trust you? Are you legit?                   | Hero, logo, short intro, brand tone          |
| 2. What is this?           | What is being offered here?                       | Headline, subheadline, clear CTA             |
| 3. Why should I care?      | Is this relevant to me? Will it solve my problem? | Benefits, features, differentiators          |
| 4. Who else is doing it?   | Do people like me trust this?                     | Testimonials, reviews, logos, case studies   |
| 5. When / how do I get it? | How much effort? How fast?                        | Process, timeline, what's included, CTA, FAQ |

## Practical rules

- Put the value proposition at the top, above the fold.
- The page should load in 2-3 seconds and look good on mobile (flag this to the designer; don't promise numbers you can't back).
- Make button text and form fields clearly readable. Keep forms short.
- No pop-ups.
- Use fewer words and more visuals: images, video, diagrams, mockups, process bars.
- Show what the visitor will get: highlight the end result.
- For bottom-of-funnel offers (book a consultation, demo, free audit, "let's talk", schedule a call), explicitly answer "What do I get?" and "How long does it take?"

## Standard flow for a page that converts

1. Hook: headline (what is this?)
2. Subheadline: short benefit (why care?)
3. Primary CTA
4. Social proof (who else?)
5. Details: process, deliverables, FAQ (how easy, when?)
6. Final CTA with a clear direction

## Section flows by page type

**Homepage for a service business**
Hero (headline, subheadline, CTA, one trust line) → the problem → what we offer (benefit cards) → offer formats (for whom / what you get / timeline / outcome) → why us → proof → process → FAQ → final CTA + short form → footer.

**Campaign or launch landing page**
Hero → problem/promise → what you get → proof → how it works → FAQ → final CTA. Minimal navigation, a single action.

**Service or product page**
Outcome headline → who it's for → what's included → process and timeline → proof specific to this service → pricing guidance or "on request" → FAQ → CTA.

## Writing patterns that work

- **Headline formulas** (adapt, don't copy): outcome + audience ("Une présence en ligne qui rassure vos clients avant même votre premier appel"), outcome + timeframe, problem → relief.
- **Benefit pattern:** feature → "so that" → business result.
- **Process pattern:** 3-5 named steps, a time estimate per step, what the client needs to provide at each step.
- **FAQ pattern:** the 5-6 objections that stop people from contacting: cost, timeline, what they must provide, ownership, support after launch, languages.

## Measuring

Track page views, form submissions, and conversion rate = (form submissions ÷ page views) × 100. Suggest an A/B test on the headline first, since it has the largest effect.

## Accuracy guardrails specific to marketing copy

- Replace guarantees and superlatives with specific, checkable statements ("designed to be fast and search-friendly" instead of "guaranteed #1 on Google").
- Never invent testimonials, client names, metrics, awards, certifications, or prices. Use placeholders.
- Keep competitor comparisons factual and checkable, or leave them out.
