# Website Content Studio

Write website content that matches what the client actually wants, borrows what works from the best sites in their field, and never states anything that isn't true. The three failure modes this skill exists to prevent:

1. **Generic copy** that could belong to any business, because nobody asked about the vision.
2. **Copied or derivative copy**, because inspiration was treated as a template to paste rather than a pattern to learn from.
3. **Confident but invented claims** (fake numbers, fake testimonials, made-up guarantees), which damage the client's credibility the moment a visitor checks.

Work through the phases in order. Each one feeds the next, and skipping discovery is the most common reason copy has to be redone.

## Phase 1: Interview the user (always the first step)

**Your first reply when this skill triggers is a set of questions, not content.** Always interview the user, every time, even when they shared a URL or a brief. Material they provide lets you ask sharper questions and skip what's already answered, but it never replaces the conversation: only the person knows the vision, the ambition, and what they would be proud to say. Copy written without hearing it is generic, and generic copy is the failure this skill exists to prevent.

How to run the interview:

1. **Read first, briefly.** Open any URLs, Notion pages or files the user gave (fetch them yourself). Spend a minute, not a research phase.
2. **Open with one line** saying what you understood so far (e.g. "I read your site and the landing-page notes; before I write, I need your vision on a few things").
3. **Ask the questions using the AskUserQuestion tool** when it is available (otherwise plain text). Ask 4 questions per round, each with 2-4 concrete options plus the user's free-text "Other", so answering is fast. Cover, over one to three rounds:
   - **Round 1, the idea:** what the business does and the single outcome clients get; who the ideal client is and what is going wrong for them today; the one action a visitor should take.
   - **Round 2, the difference and the people:** what truly sets them apart (ask for the concrete detail behind each claim: what is in the deliverable, what is fixed, how the method works); who is behind the business and what they believe; what proof exists today (testimonials, results, work, logos) or "none yet".
   - **Round 3, voice and limits:** tone (3 adjectives, plus a brand that sounds right and one that sounds wrong); languages; pages needed; prices shown or hidden; words or claims to avoid.
     Use `references/discovery-questions.md` for wording and defaults. Skip a question only when the user has already answered it clearly in this conversation. If answers raise new gaps, ask a short follow-up round rather than guessing.
4. **Reflect it back** as the Vision Brief below, in the user's own words where possible, and ask one confirmation question: "Is this the vision? Anything to change before I research and write?" Do not research or write until they confirm.

If the user is genuinely unreachable (a scheduled or unattended run), stop at the brief: deliver the Vision Brief with your open questions and proposed defaults as the result, instead of writing full copy on guesses.

### Vision Brief template

```
Business: [what they do, in one sentence]
Audience: [who they are, what they worry about, what they search for]
Core promise: [the outcome the client gets]
Who is behind it: [founder/team names, background, point of view: needed for a trust section]
Stage: [established / new with no portfolio / relaunch: decides what proof exists]
Differentiators: [2-4 true reasons to choose this business, each with a concrete detail]
Tone: [3 adjectives + one "never sounds like" example]
Primary action: [the ONE thing a visitor should do]
Languages: [FR / EN / both]
Proof available: [testimonials, results, case studies, logos, numbers: or "none yet"]
Constraints: [pricing visibility, legal wording, words to avoid]
```

## Phase 2: Gather facts and inspiration (keep them separate)

Two kinds of sources do different jobs. Mixing them is how invented claims sneak in.

**Fact sources** describe the client truthfully: their current site, brief, Notion pages, documents, the user's own statements. Only these can support claims about the business. Record where each fact came from.

**Inspiration sources** show how good content is built in this space: best-in-class sites in the same industry or audience, strong competitors, well-converting landing pages, review sites and forums where the audience describes their own problems in their own words. Use web search to find 4-6 of them, and read them rather than guessing.

From inspiration, extract **patterns, not text**:

- How the headline states the outcome, and its rough length
- Section order and what each section answers
- How they present proof, pricing, and process
- The audience's own vocabulary (this is gold for headlines and FAQs)
- What competitors all say (so you can say something different)

Never reproduce sentences from inspiration sources. Beyond the copyright problem, borrowed copy sounds borrowed and cannot be backed up as the client's own claims. Write a short **Inspiration Notes** section: each source, the one thing worth learning from it, and how it shapes this project.

## Phase 3: Plan the page

Choose the structure before writing sentences. Read `references/conversion-principles.md`: it holds the landing-page principles (one CTA, value proposition first, the 5 W's, benefits over features, show what they get) and the standard section flows for a homepage, landing page, and multi-page site. Decide:

- Which page type this is, and the single action it drives
- Which sections it needs, in order, and which of the 5 W's each answers
- Where proof is strong and where it is missing (missing proof becomes a placeholder, never an invention)

## Phase 4: Write the content

Write for the confirmed audience in the confirmed tone. Principles that matter most:

- **Outcome first.** The headline says what the visitor gets, not what the company is. Headlines under about 10 words; subheadlines under about 25.
- **Benefit, then feature.** "Your site works on any phone, so you never lose an inquiry to a broken page" beats "Responsive design."
- **Fewer words, more structure.** Short sentences, one idea per section, and a note wherever an icon, image, mockup, or process bar should replace text.
- **One primary CTA**, repeated at the hero, midpoint, and end, with the same wording each time.
- **Specific over superlative.** "Pages that load in under 3 seconds" is believable; "the best website ever" is not.
- **People are proof.** If the business is new or has little portfolio, the people behind it are the strongest trust signal. Include a short "who we are" section (founder note, point of view, or manifesto) and ask for names, background and a belief the visitor can remember. Never skip this because it is awkward to write.
- **Every differentiator carries a concrete detail.** "Strategy first", "senior attention" and "fixed scope" are claims most competitors make, so nobody remembers them. Back each one with something specific and true from the client's real method: what is inside the strategy document, what the launch kit contains, what exactly is fixed. If the client has not given that detail, ask for it or placeholder it. Do not leave the generic version in.
- **The headline says what you do.** Wordplay or the brand name alone is not enough. A first-time visitor should know the offer and the outcome from the headline plus subheadline, and the pain should be named directly where possible.
- **Don't presume the visitor's situation.** Write for the range of people in the audience (for example founders with a finished product and those still pre-product, or with a rough brand to fix). Where the audience includes both, address both.
- **Plain, unambiguous wording.** Replace phrases a reader could take two ways ("no pitch deck" becomes "we won't sell you anything on the call"). Drop name rationales and metaphors that need explaining.
- **Bilingual means native in both.** When FR and EN are both requested, write each natively with the same structure. Do not translate word for word.

For anything the client has not supplied (testimonials, metrics, prices, guarantees, client names, timelines), insert a clearly marked placeholder such as `[TESTIMONIAL: client name, role, quote]` or `[RESULT: metric to confirm]`. A visible gap is a to-do for the client; an invented claim is a liability.

## Phase 5: Accuracy check before delivering

Review your own draft the way a skeptical visitor and the client's lawyer would.

1. Build a **Claim Ledger**: every factual claim in the copy (numbers, timelines, "we offer X", "trusted by Y", guarantees) with its status: `Verified` (source named), `Client-provided` (the user said so), or `Placeholder` (needs confirmation). Anything that fits none of these gets removed or rewritten as a softer true statement.
2. Check the copy against the Vision Brief: audience, tone, single CTA, differentiators all present?
3. Check that no sentence is lifted from an inspiration source.
4. Check that FR and EN say the same thing and that the placeholders match.
5. Run the quality pass in `references/content-quality-checks.md` (people section present, differentiators specific, headline clear, no presumed audience state, no unsourced soft claims, no ambiguous phrases).
6. Give every placeholder an **empty-state plan** (see below), because bracketed text cannot ship in a real layout.

## Deliverable

Give the user one document they can read, edit and share, in this order:

1. **Vision Brief** (with any assumptions flagged)
2. **Inspiration Notes** (sources and what was learned)
3. **Page content**, section by section: copy (FR and/or EN), a one-line visual or layout note, and which of the 5 W's it answers
4. **Claim Ledger**
5. **Open items and empty-state plan**: every placeholder the client must fill, in priority order, and for each one the design decision if it stays empty: hide the section, show a "coming soon" state, or ship with a fallback line. Testimonial and work/portfolio sections always need this plan.
6. **Optional extras**: 3 alternative headlines for A/B testing, and the metrics to track (page views, form submissions, conversion rate = submissions ÷ views × 100)

End by naming the one or two open items that would most improve conversion if filled. Do not recap the steps you took.
